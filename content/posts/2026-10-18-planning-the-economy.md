Your first Tesland delivery gets you moving. A few more get you thinking: keep saving for the next city, buy a Cybercab that earns alongside you, or spend some cash making your car your own? We modeled those choices in five-minute slices, then followed drivers all the way from San Francisco to their first home—and, eventually, a shared launch to Mars.

Deliveries, cash-based levels, saved vehicles and a game-wide Mars goal give the career its foundation. Next we want to add income-producing cabs and property. The simulations below explore how those investments could change the journey.

## The first big purchase

Suppose you start with $2,500 and earn $60,000 an hour delivering. After about 23 minutes you have enough for a $25,000 Cybercab. Buying it comes with a $5,000 achievement award, so your wallet drops by $20,000. Now your cab starts earning alongside you.

If that first cab earns for 75% of your active play, it brings in $11,250 per active hour. About two hours after the purchase, your wallet catches up with the driver who kept saving. After that, the cab is still earning. Move the controls below to see what changes when it works less, the cab costs more, or you spring for a cosmetic first.

That dip in cash is the interesting part. Buying a cab makes you poorer for a while and gives you a chance to earn faster later. Buying paint gives you a different kind of return: you get to drive something you like. Both deserve a place in the game.

## Your wallet is your level

We considered an XP system, then chose a simpler direction: **your current cash balance determines your level**. Earn enough and the Bay opens up. Spend enough and your level can fall until you earn it back. Achievements and purchases stay yours.

Oakland and Berkeley open at $10k, the Peninsula and East Bay at $50k, and San Jose at $250k. If spending drops your balance below a gate, you can leave the area; an inaccessible delivery is rerouted. The Vault shows the cash and level a purchase will leave you with.

For the larger economy, we modeled these additional career goals:

| Wallet | Level | What opens |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| $0 | 0 | San Francisco, Marin and starter jobs |
| $10,000 | 1 | Oakland |
| $50,000 | 2 | Fremont and SFO |
| $250,000 | 3 | San Jose |
| $500,000 / $1m | 4 / 5 | Higher-paying contracts |
| $2m | 6 | Property market and advanced contracts |
| $4m / $8m | 7 / 8 | Luxury and estate contracts |

The model also proposes separate gates for Berkeley at $20k, San Mateo at $75k, Palo Alto at $100k and Mountain View at $150k. The current regional unlock achievements pay once, even if you drop a level and unlock the region again.

## From one cab to a fleet

In the model, the first five Cybercabs cost **$25k, $50k, $100k, $200k and $400k**. Their ownership achievements return $5k, $10k, $20k, $40k and $80k respectively. Each active cab has the same $15k-per-hour base earning rate, so a bigger fleet pays more, while each successive purchase takes longer to earn back its price. The model pays cabs while their owner is playing.

We want owners to find their cars on the map, follow them through the city, and put their name and colors on them. A driver and an owner should each be identifiable when they are different people. The garage holds personalized car configurations; dispatched Cybercabs can stay out earning.

## A place of your own

Our proposed property market opens at a $2m wallet threshold, after a career of deliveries and fleet decisions. A ready-built starter home would cost $1m. Or buy a $400k plot and spend $600k building a home to your taste. An owner could always reach and build on their own plot, even if a purchase lowers their balance below the market threshold.

The planned home comes with two garage spaces and a free teleport back home. Extra spaces cost $50k, $100k, $200k, $400k and $800k. Personal chargers start at $25k, and a launch pad or thermal starts at $50k. We want upgrades to change how a place feels to use: pull a favorite car out of the garage, recharge, then launch back toward the city.

We propose bigger plots and builds in $2m, $4m, $8m and $16m starting packages. Players could own one ready-built home bought from the city or another player, plus homes they build on additional plots. Starter plots should always be available; a limited number of special world locations give players something to hunt for. Players would set their own resale prices. On a $2m sale, a 5% fee removes $100k and the seller receives $1.9m.

For players who want to keep building, the property career can grow upward. A development plot costs $800k and its foundation and first floor cost $1.2m. The next floor costs $200k, with each additional floor about 10% more than the last. By the twentieth floor you've spent $12.232m on land and construction, before achievement awards. Milestones at floors 1, 5, 10 and 20 pay once, and the building remains yours even when its cost drops your wallet level. We haven't assumed any rental income: a tower is a place to make your mark and, eventually, a property another player might want to buy.

That fee, a city-sold plot, a construction bill and a shop cosmetic are *money sinks*: cash leaves the economy when players buy them. Deliveries, cab earnings and achievement awards create cash. If more money chases a fixed supply of desirable plots, prices can rise; attractive things to buy give that money somewhere to go. We'll watch earnings, spending and actual sale prices as the market takes shape.

We want to grow those stops into delivery-led programs. After 20 deliveries, a driver could carry clean-energy equipment at Berkeley, housing prototypes for UCSF, motor parts at Stanford, or flight equipment at SFO. The university programs would award five degrees after 2, 6, 12, 20 and 30 qualifying program deliveries; SFO would use 1, 4, 8, 14 and 20. Each chain pays $15.5k across its five milestones and opens a related upgrade or livery along the way. At six minutes per job, the 30 program deliveries take about three active hours after the initial 20-delivery prerequisite. That's a reason to learn a part of the Bay while continuing the delivery career.

Airport runs deserve a fare that makes the extra drive worthwhile. In our level-four example, a $2k SFO supplement brings a seven-minute airport job to about $111k per active hour, just above the $110k rate for six-minute regular deliveries. The five airport awards make the first 20 jobs more rewarding still.

Rocket Dome, racing and parking are for the occasional detour and achievement hunt. A Dome win could give everyone in the room 1.5× eligible earnings for ten minutes. A separate global event could give everyone 2× for 30 minutes; together the bonuses cap at 3×. They apply to deliveries, active cab income and eligible activity pay. Achievement awards keep their normal value.

## What the journeys look like

We ran 250 seeded versions of seven delivery-led strategies, each for 40 active hours. The first three share driving and travel assumptions to compare saving, buying one cab and reinvesting in a fleet; those choices can also change later jobs. The other paths vary spending and play style. Events are off in this comparison, and the hours are time spent playing.

| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Delivery saver | $7,240 | 17.4 h |
| First cab | $8,101 | 16.0 h |
| Fleet reinvestor | $9,030 | 14.8 h |
| Customizer | $7,101 | 16.7 h |
| Delivery explorer | $6,936 | 17.7 h |
| 30-minute casual | $5,624 | 21.9 h |

The fleet reinvestor reaches a first home about two and a half hours before the saver, even though each cab purchase temporarily lowers the wallet. During the first hour, that driver earns $9,030 gross per five minutes but adds only $2,691 to the wallet after purchases and fines. Construction happens as soon as the driver can afford it. The simulations assume a steady supply of jobs and cabs earning during active play; traffic, charging and downtime will change the result.

Each path chooses two research programs. Once eligible, half its deliveries can advance a chosen program; SFO jobs add a minute and a $2k supplement. The model assigns jobs abstractly, so we'll need to check those travel assumptions in the Bay itself.

## Give drivers reasons to keep going

Achievements should point toward the next interesting thing: an on-time delivery, a new city, a new drop-off, a clean driving streak, or the first cab earning back its cost. Each pays once. Delivery milestones at 1, 10, 25, 50 and 100 jobs give the longer journey landmarks of its own, with smaller check-ins between them and at 250.

The redesigned milestones bring the longest achievement-free stretch in the first two hours to **20–25 minutes at the 90th percentile**. In hours two through eight, the longest gaps fall from 60–85 minutes to **45–60 minutes**; players who skip research still see 50–65 minutes. The opening remains busy: **51–80%** of early half-hour blocks contain more than four individual awards. Group awards from the same action into one notification and that falls to **7–49%** of blocks. Those are two different measures: grouping makes the messages calmer while each earned award still pays.

We also tried rotating delivery contracts—two on-time jobs, three clean jobs, two distinct stops. They give visible progress between bigger milestones. In the model, they replace a $750 checkpoint paid every three deliveries with $650 per completed contract; completions arrived slightly less often. That's a useful next playtest, alongside airport and university goals that ask players to do something different.

## The long road to Mars

<figure>
<img src="/blog/economy/starship-concept.webp" alt="A Starship and Super Heavy rocket ready for launch at a Fremont spaceport as drivers gather nearby." loading="lazy">
<figcaption>A Fremont launch is the destination for the whole Bay. Concept art for the Starship finale.</figcaption>

Every eligible delivery and achievement moves the shared Mars meter while the earnings stay in the driver's wallet. Once players together earn **$1 billion**, Starship and Super Heavy are ready at the Fremont spaceport. Drivers can board for Mars and continue exploring afterwards. We want a future finale to honor everyone who helped and give the season a proper sendoff.

How long might that take? In a [community run](https://github.com/swyxio/TeslaRoadster6/blob/main/docs/STARSHIP_ECONOMY.md#a-population-run-using-the-delivery-simulations) using our saver, cab, fleet and casual paths, **50 players each playing an hour a day** reach $100m on day 17, $500m on day 105 and $1 billion on day 223. Ten players on that schedule take 1,162 days; 250 players playing half an hour a day reach the goal on day 70. The run keeps the same players throughout and extends their later earning rates after 40 hours. Player activity and earning rates matter as much as the number on the meter.

Open the Mars mission menu to follow the global total. Earnings count automatically across rooms; there's no need to spend your cash to fund the launch.
