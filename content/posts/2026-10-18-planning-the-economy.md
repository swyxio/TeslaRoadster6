Your first Tesland delivery gets you moving. A few more get you thinking: keep saving for the next city, buy a Cybercab that earns alongside you, or spend some cash making your car your own? We modeled those choices in five-minute slices, then followed drivers all the way from San Francisco to their first home—and, eventually, a shared launch to Mars.

Today you can deliver around the Bay and contribute to a room's Mars mission. The persistent fleet, property market and game-wide milestone in this article are the economy we want to build next.

## The first big purchase

Suppose you start with $2,500 and earn $60,000 an hour delivering. After about 23 minutes you have enough for a $25,000 Cybercab. Buying it comes with a $5,000 achievement award, so your wallet drops by $20,000. Now your cab starts earning alongside you.

If that first cab earns for 75% of your active play, it brings in $11,250 per active hour. About two hours after the purchase, your wallet catches up with the driver who kept saving. After that, the cab is still earning. Move the controls below to see what changes when it works less, the cab costs more, or you spring for a cosmetic first.

That dip in cash is the interesting part. Buying a cab makes you poorer for a while and gives you a chance to earn faster later. Buying paint gives you a different kind of return: you get to drive something you like. Both deserve a place in the game.

## Your wallet is your level

We considered an XP system, then chose the simpler option: **your current cash balance determines your level**. Earn enough and the Bay opens up. Spend enough and your level can fall until you earn it back. Achievements and purchases stay yours.

A driver who spends below a city threshold can finish a job already in progress and take local contracts. Re-entering later may require rebuilding the balance. We should show the level a purchase will leave you with before you buy it.

| Wallet | Level | What opens |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| $0 | 0 | San Francisco, Marin and starter jobs |
| $10,000 | 1 | Oakland |
| $50,000 | 2 | Fremont and SFO |
| $250,000 | 3 | San Jose |
| $500,000 / $1m | 4 / 5 | Higher-paying contracts |
| $2m | 6 | Property market and advanced contracts |
| $4m / $8m | 7 / 8 | Luxury and estate contracts |

Berkeley opens at $20k, San Mateo at $75k, Palo Alto at $100k and Mountain View at $150k. A first city unlock pays an achievement once, even if you drop a level and unlock it again.

## From one cab to a fleet

The first five Cybercabs cost **$25k, $50k, $100k, $200k and $400k**. Their ownership achievements return $5k, $10k, $20k, $40k and $80k respectively. Each active cab has the same $15k-per-hour base earning rate, so a bigger fleet pays more, while each successive purchase takes longer to earn back its price. The model pays cabs while their owner is playing.

We want owners to find their cars on the map, follow them through the city, and put their name and colors on them. A driver and an owner should each be identifiable when they are different people. The garage holds personalized car configurations; dispatched Cybercabs can stay out earning.

In the expanded economy, there's plenty to spend on before buying a house. Paint starts at $2k, decals and lettering at $5k, wheels and trim at $15k, wraps and fleet branding at $50k, and premium effects at $150k. Early vehicle and flight kits start at $10k, $20k and $40k. Ordinary cars remain available to drive. We also want to bring more EVs to the Bay: BYDs and Rivians, plus the Lucid Air, Hyundai IONIQ, Kia EV9, Chevrolet Silverado EV and Ford F-150 Lightning.

## A place of your own

After building a career of deliveries and fleet decisions, the $2m wallet threshold opens the property market. A ready-built starter home costs $1m. Or buy a $400k plot and spend $600k building a home to your taste. An owner can always reach and build on their own plot, even if a purchase lowers their balance below the market threshold.

A home comes with two garage spaces and a free teleport back home. Extra spaces cost $50k, $100k, $200k, $400k and $800k. Personal chargers start at $25k, and a launch pad or thermal starts at $50k. We want upgrades to change how a place feels to use: pull a favorite car out of the garage, recharge, then launch back toward the city.

Bigger plots and builds come in $2m, $4m, $8m and $16m starting packages. You can own one ready-built home bought from the city or another player, plus homes you build yourself on additional plots. Starter plots should always be available; a limited number of special world locations give players something to hunt for. Players set their own resale prices. On a $2m sale, a 5% fee removes $100k and the seller receives $1.9m.

That fee, a city-sold plot, a construction bill and a shop cosmetic are *money sinks*: cash leaves the economy when players buy them. Deliveries, cab earnings and achievement awards create cash. If more money chases a fixed supply of desirable plots, prices can rise; attractive things to buy give that money somewhere to go. We'll watch earnings, spending and actual sale prices as the market takes shape.

## A Bay worth exploring

The next delivery can take you somewhere new, and the place should give you a reason to come back. Start with the blue research rings already in the game: stop at UCSF for $20 and a repair discount, Berkeley for $35 and a motor discount, Stanford for $50 and a nozzle discount, or SFO for $40 and a tank discount. Your first delivery to SFO earns an extra $80. These rewards and discounts last for the drive.

We want to grow those stops into delivery-led programs. After 20 deliveries, a driver could carry energy equipment at Berkeley, medical supplies for UCSF, flight-research equipment at Stanford, or airport cargo at SFO. The university programs would award five degrees after 2, 6, 12, 20 and 30 qualifying program deliveries; SFO would use 1, 4, 8, 14 and 20. Each chain pays $15.5k across its five milestones and opens a related upgrade or livery along the way. At six minutes per job, the 30 program deliveries take about three active hours after the initial 20-delivery prerequisite. That's a reason to learn a part of the Bay while continuing the delivery career.

Airport runs deserve a fare that makes the extra drive worthwhile. In our level-four example, a $2k SFO supplement brings a seven-minute airport job to about $111k per active hour, just above the $110k rate for six-minute regular deliveries. The five airport awards make the first 20 jobs more rewarding still.

Rocket Dome, racing and parking are for the occasional detour and achievement hunt. A Dome win could give everyone in the room 1.5× eligible earnings for ten minutes. A separate global event could give everyone 2× for 30 minutes; together the bonuses cap at 3×. They apply to deliveries, active cab income and eligible activity pay. Achievement awards keep their normal value.

## What the journeys look like

We ran 250 seeded versions of seven delivery-led strategies, each for 40 active hours. The first three share driving and travel assumptions to compare saving, buying one cab and reinvesting in a fleet; those choices can also change later jobs. The other paths vary spending and play style. Events are off in this comparison, and the hours are time spent playing.

| Path | First-hour gross / 5 min | Median first home |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Delivery saver | $7,279 | 17.7 h |
| First cab | $8,179 | 16.2 h |
| Fleet reinvestor | $9,087 | 15.2 h |
| Customizer | $7,185 | 17.0 h |
| Delivery explorer | $6,981 | 17.7 h |
| 30-minute casual | $5,648 | 21.7 h |
| Mixed economy | $6,251 | 18.4 h |

In this model, the fleet reinvestor reaches a first home about two and a half hours before the saver, even though each cab purchase temporarily lowers the wallet. Construction happens as soon as the driver can afford it. The simulations assume a steady supply of jobs and cabs earning during active play; traffic, charging and downtime will change the result.

These seven journeys model the core delivery loop. The university and SFO programs have their own rate calculations: for example, the final university tier asks for ten more jobs, or an hour at six minutes per job. We want to try them with the existing achievements before treating the cadence as fixed.

## Give drivers reasons to keep going

Achievements should point toward the next interesting thing: an on-time delivery, a new city, a new drop-off, a clean driving streak, or the first cab earning back its cost. Each pays once. Delivery milestones at 1, 10, 50, 100 and 250 jobs give the longer journey landmarks of its own.

The new milestone set shortened the longest achievement-free stretch in the first two hours to **15–25 minutes at the 90th percentile**, down from 25–45 minutes. It also made the opening too busy: **61–88% of early half-hour blocks contain more than four achievements**. Later, the longest gaps in hours two through eight still reach **60–85 minutes at the 90th percentile**. We need to spread meaningful goals across the journey and group notifications earned by the same action.

We also tried rotating delivery contracts—two on-time jobs, three clean jobs, two distinct stops. They give visible progress between bigger milestones. In the model, they replace a $750 checkpoint paid every three deliveries with $650 per completed contract; completions arrived slightly less often. That's a useful next playtest, alongside airport and university goals that ask players to do something different.

## The long road to Mars

<img src="/blog/economy/starship-concept.webp" alt="A Starship and Super Heavy rocket ready for launch at a Fremont spaceport as drivers gather nearby." loading="lazy">
<figcaption>A Fremont launch is the destination for the whole Bay. Concept art for the Starship finale.</figcaption>
</figure>

Imagine every delivery, cab fare and achievement moving the same giant meter. Once players together earn **$1 billion**, Starship and Super Heavy are ready at the Fremont spaceport. Everyone who helped is honored; players board for Mars and the room gets a proper finale. The earnings still belong to the players who made them.

How long might that take? In a [community run](https://github.com/swyxio/TeslaRoadster6/blob/main/docs/STARSHIP_ECONOMY.md#a-population-run-using-the-delivery-simulations) using our saver, cab, fleet and casual paths, **50 players each playing an hour a day** reach $100m on day 17, $500m on day 91 and $1 billion on day 187. Ten players on that schedule take 949 days; 250 players playing half an hour a day reach the goal on day 67. The run keeps the same players throughout and extends their later earning rates after 40 hours. The goal needs to feel distant at the scale people actually play, so player activity and earning rates matter as much as the number on the meter.

There's already a Mars mission in the game: open the mission menu, contribute from your session cash and watch your room's progress bar fill. At $1 billion, reach the Fremont spaceport to board. The room fund persists; solo progress lasts for that drive. The game-wide earnings meter will need persistent identities and a reliable way to count each reward once.
